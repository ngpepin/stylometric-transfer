# Lithonia Lighting Case **Analysis**

Lithonia Lighting's pioneering efforts in leveraging information technology for strategic advantage have resulted in high profitability and rapid sales growth in the commercial and industrial builders segment during the 1980s. However, the recent economic recession and consequent decline in building projects requiring lighting products threaten to make price the dominant basis of competition in the industry. Lithonia management is concerned that the large investments required to sustain the operation and development of Light*Link will lock the company into a high-cost position, rendering it unable to respond to heightened price pressures.

## 1. **Problem Statement**

Management at Lithonia Lighting must reconsider its information technology and interorganizational information system (IOS) strategy in light of the recent economic downturn, and determine what, if any, steps should be taken to defend and sustain the competitive advantage that IT has provided the company over the last decade.

## Situation Analysis

### Strategic Impact

Prior to 1991, Lithonia Lighting, a subsidiary of NSI, had experienced steady and rapid growth dating back to the 1960s, largely funded through retained earnings. Buyouts and consolidations in the 1970s and 1980s reduced the field of competition from 1,300 to nine major companies. Lithonia built its business around a network of independent agents, whose role was to create demand for the company's products by aggressively lobbying key decision makers throughout the building design and construction process (e.g., architects, electrical engineers, distributors, and sub-contractors).

The oil crisis of 1973 resulted in a high demand for lighting products and control systems which reduced electricity consumption. Further encouraged by the imposition of strict government conservation regulations, lighting equipment manufacturers like Lithonia raced to develop sophisticated low wattage products and installation systems and strategies.

Growing product complexity increased the information intensity of the industry. To provide quality service, for example, sales agents had to broaden their skill base to include knowledge of lighting engineering. Other stakeholders, like architects, engineers, and specifiers, required more detailed product specifications and design assistance. Lithonia decided in 1980 to provide this value-added information to its stakeholders in the form of a marketing and logistics IOS, called "Light*Link", for which it is both content and conduit prime.

It is believed that Lithonia's growth in the 1980s was largely due to market differentiation and increased stakeholder value perception provided by Light*Link. In terms of the strategic relevance of their information systems, Lithonia has seen itself residing in the "strategic" quadrant (see fig. 1): that is, IT is critical to their current daily operations and continued application development is critical to future success. But Lithonia needs a good IT strategy, considerable planning, and senior management support to follow through. This presents a problem for the company because of the current economic crisis and because IT strategy and planning are not performed rigorously.

# Organizational Learning & IT

Lithonia is far more sophisticated in its use of IT than its competitors. This is an advantage in terms of business performance; however, it is also a disadvantage in that Lithonia has had to make the pioneering investments in IT.

## Sourcing Policy

All systems have been developed in-house and all equipment is owned by Lithonia. The company will eventually have to upgrade its hardware and software, necessitating further IT investments. Refer to Exhibit B for a list of the benefits and challenges of outsourcing.

## Application Development Process

All application development is done in-house by the IS group, led by Charles Darnell, "a visionary." Systems were typically late and sometimes delivered less than what had been promised. Users had to be content with the promise of improvements in the upgrade releases. Darnell often envisioned and described projects several years in advance; he also had a tendency to overcommit. The IS shop was a lot larger than the IT shops of most of Lithonia's competitors, with 130 people in 1990. Darnell believes that Light*Link holds the potential of completely changing the way the lighting industry does business. Refer to Exhibit C for an information systems risk-return portfolio.

### **Partnerships**

The IS group management lives on the leading edge of technology. Darnell, the senior VP, reports to the CEO. General management has supported IT development all along but is now cautious and is having difficulty justifying continued IT growth in these economic times, especially when all areas are being asked to cut back. Functional management is skeptical of IT management's promises and is probably not often consulted about their needs when Darnell is developing new applications. The partnerships are not very strategic or united when it comes to IT, which is an area where teamwork is particularly important.

## 3. Identification of Alternatives

- PROCEED WITH DARNELL'S CURRENT IT PLAN AS IS.
- DISCONTINUE FURTHER SYSTEM DEVELOPMENT ACTIVITIES UNTIL ECONOMIC CONDITIONS AND EARNINGS IMPROVE.
- REDIRECT RESOURCES TO IMPROVING INTERNAL SYSTEMS, RATHER THAN COMMITTING TO INCREASING VALUE FOR EXTERNAL IOS PARTNERS.

This alternative represents a certain departure from the stakeholder partnership concept that has inspired Light*Link's design from the outset. However, it would respond to Conway and Naramore's concerns.

- DIVEST OF, OR LICENSE AND OUTSOURCE, LIGHT*LINK.
- PERFORM A VALUE CHAIN ANALYSIS TO IDENTIFY KEY PARTNER NEEDS, AND THE MANNER IN WHICH THEY CAN BEST BE MET ORGANIZATIONALLY; ALSO DEVELOP A DETAILED IT ARCHITECTURE (EXHIBIT A).

Light*Link's evolution and design has been driven more by technological considerations than by a sharp focus on customer (i.e., partner) needs. Partners should be willing to assume an appropriate portion of the cost of developing and operating Light*Link if the IOS' features offer distinct added value to them. Non value-added activities should be abandoned to conserve or redirect resources.

- OPEN LIGHT*LINK UP TO COMPETITORS AND COMPLEMENTARY SUPPLIERS,

Pricing membership rates or per-transaction fees in a fashion which provides a disincentive to competitors for developing their own IOS, but which nonetheless contributes towards paying for the operation of the system. Focus IT efforts on using the information and intelligence obtained from the IOS in market segmentation, product and service development, and pricing decisions rather than on attempts to dominate the increasingly "neutral ground" of information distribution. This alternative argues against McClung's assumption that, in the lighting industry, "IT probably isn't going to make [too] much difference," by attaching importance to IT's potential for contributing to differentiation based on service and quality.

## 4. Evaluation of Alternatives

Exhibit E provides a full analysis of the alternatives described in the previous section. Recommendation: Lithonia should pursue alternatives "E" and "F".

Just as American Airlines was able in the 1980s to use the information from its SABRE IOS to successfully respond to intense price competition from startup companies entering the newly-deregulated airlines industry, Lithonia should not abandon or neglect its IOS, but instead use it as a stepping stone to a higher level of competitive advantage based on market intelligence. Like AA, Lithonia must align its resources towards the development of a core capability and sustainable competitive advantage in the analysis of information rather than the monopolization of the information conduit (i.e., it should implement alternative "F"). It is crucial for Lithonia to fully recognize the necessity of letting go of direct control over its IOS during this second stage of the information intensification of the lighting industry. As Malone, Yates, and Benjamin argue in their May-June 1989 article in the Harvard Business Review, "Early developers of single-source or biased sales channels should plan for the transition to unbiased electronic markets." [p. 168]

"...truly useful computer systems are becoming too big and too expensive for any one company to build and own; joint ventures will become the rule rather than the exception... The next arena for competitive differentiation revolves around the intensification of analysis. Astute managers will shift their attention from systems to information."

— Max D. Hopper, senior vice president of Information Systems at American Airlines and architect of AA's SABRE reservation system (Source: Revolutions in Real Time, Harvard Business Review, 1990, pp. 117-118.)

Planning for this transition entails altering the company's IT strategy from a focus on the development of a *proprietary* marketing and logistics IOS, to the joint creation of an "industry platform" IOS, where industry partners all enjoy economies of scale and reduced costs. Lithonia IT resources should be redirected to the development of Executive Information Systems (EISs) which draw from Light*Link's prodigious storehouse of industry data to provide management with the necessary real-time information needed to be "first-movers" in developing strategies and tactics which both enhance differentiation and achieve the necessary organizational synergies to cut costs; making the pursuit of a value-priced market position possible in a period of lower demand and slimmer margins.

In our view, this approach is preferable to McClung's tacit conclusion that IT cannot play a significant role in the lighting industry [p. 363], and that one should be resigned to a "commodity" view of the lighting products industry. Clearly the evidence points in the opposite direction: IT was a key success factor for Lithonia during the 1980s when the industry was challenged with innovating energy-saving technologies and designs, and we believe that by implementing our recommendations IT could continue to be a KSF in the 1990s, when environmental, property security, and esthetic concerns will demand innovation and customization in both the physical and service components of the industry's product offerings.

This transition must, however, be preceded by a thorough value-chain analysis and the development of an IT strategy, as described in alternative "E". The company could also elect to outsource the systems-level operation of the IOS, as this activity is not necessary to the development of a greater analytical capability.

### **Factors to Consider in Outsourcing***

#### **Benefits**

- Freeing up management and other resources
- Sharing costs
- Getting rid of obsolete hardware
- Creating integrated networks
- Freeing up capital
- Avoiding cost overruns
- Accessing new value-added services
- Training staff
- Offsetting replacement cost
- Spreading costs over many years
- Continually innovating
- Reducing staff levels
- Interfacing with other information systems

#### **Challenges**

- Maintaining confidentiality
- Retaining control
- Achieving goals other than efficiency
- Confronting transition problems
- Legal liability
- Maintaining continuity of service

# Information Systems Risk/Return Portfolio

#### **Risks**

- Lithonia spends a lot more on IT than its competitors do
- Lithonia rarely has the lowest price products (IT systems have been partly responsible for this, but IT is still within 2% of sales)
- When the markets are down their overhead is more than that of their competitors
- IT in the lighting industry will never be truly strategic as a lot of business will still be conducted by small companies without the IT resources
- Is the previous means by which systems have been developed still appropriate in these economic times?
- Costs of pioneering

#### **Return**

- The IT investments have clearly allowed Lithonia and its agents to profitably handle the rapid growth in sales volume and product diversity
- Lithonia has first mover advantages
